1. Executive summary

The scorecard ranks risk consistently in and out of time: its Gini is 0.310 on the hold-out test sample, 0.316 on 2014 loans and 0.330 on 2015 loans, with no decay. Ten features carry it, all with the expected sign, all significant, with variance inflation factors below 2. On the development window it is well calibrated (Hosmer–Lemeshow p = 0.65; no master-scale grade rejected).

Out of time, calibration fails. Default rates rose inside every Lending Club grade after 2013, and the model under-predicts the 2015 vintage by two percentage points (mean PD 12.9%, observed 14.9%); the Jeffreys test rejects the PD of seven of eight grades. The population stability index never left green (score PSI ≤ 0.026 in every quarter), so standard population monitoring would not have caught this. The applicants looked the same on paper and defaulted more often. Recalibrating the intercept on 2014 outcomes, as an annual review would have done, closes only a third of the 2015 gap, because conditions kept deteriorating.

The LightGBM challenger adds about four Gini points in every sample (0.352 / 0.354 / 0.363). At a 20% decline rate on the 2015 book that is 628 more defaults avoided, roughly $4 million of loss on $3.6 billion lent. Its top decline reason agrees with the scorecard's only 29% of the time.

Opinion: approve the scorecard as champion, with conditions. (1) Recalibrate before use and on monitoring triggers, not on a fixed annual cycle. (2) Add an early-delinquency backtest to monitoring, because the 36-month outcome arrives too late to steer by. (3) Keep LightGBM as a challenger and benchmark until a governed reason-code framework exists for it. The findings log is in Section 10.

2. Purpose, scope and use

The model estimates the probability that a new 36-month unsecured instalment loan to an individual borrower is charged off at any point in its life (lifetime PD). It is built from information available at application only: the credit-bureau record, stated income and employment, the loan request and the debt-to-income ratio.

In scope: individual applications for 36-month loans in the product and credit-policy range of the development data (FICO 660 and above, Lending Club's minimum).

Out of scope: 60-month loans (different default timing; excluded from development), joint applications (239 in the whole 2007–2015 population, too few to model), behavioural scoring of loans already on book, and IFRS 9 / CECL provisioning. That would need a term structure of PD and forward-looking adjustments. Section 7 gives an empirical 12-month PD for the Basel-style calculation only.

3. Data

3.1 Source and lineage

The data are Lending Club's public loan-level files, 2007 to 2018Q4, as compiled on Kaggle (wordsforthewise/lending-club) and fetched from a CC0 mirror. The file is 1,675,133,810 bytes (sha256 3eae03c2…0933d1f36a, recorded in data/SOURCES.md) and contains 2,260,668 loans, which matches the published count. The raw file is never modified; lcrisk ingest derives everything from it.

3.3 Features

Features come from an explicit allowlist of fields known at application (ingest.py). Everything recorded after origination is excluded: payments, recoveries, the last FICO pull, hardship and settlement flags. Excluding by allowlist, not denylist, means a new post-origination field added to the source cannot leak in by accident. Three further groups are excluded on purpose:

  • Lending Club's grade, sub-grade and interest rate. These are the output of Lending Club's own model. Using them would make ours a copy of theirs; instead they serve as a benchmark (Section 6.4). For the same reason the instalment amount, which is computed from the interest rate, is not used.
  • Geography (state, three-digit zip). Fair-lending law treats location as a potential proxy for protected characteristics; it is left out rather than tested and defended.
  • Free text (job title, loan description). It would need its own review.

Five features are derived: credit history length at application, employment length in years, the FICO band midpoint, and two affordability ratios, loan amount and revolving balance to annual income.

3.4 Data availability and the development window

Lending Club added sixteen bureau fields (total credit limits, bankcard utilisation and headroom, mortgage accounts, recent account openings) part-way through 2012. They are absent before 2012Q3, 43% populated that quarter, and complete from 2012Q4. Developing on 2007–2013 would have meant either dropping these fields or letting their missingness stand in for the issue date. The development window is therefore 2012Q4–2013Q4, the period a lender would actually redevelop on. The earlier vintages are used only to calibrate the stress scenario (Section 8).

4. Methodology

4.1 Samples

The development window is split at random, stratified on the target, into training (70%, 80,922 loans) and test (30%, 34,681 loans). The 2014 and 2015 vintages are out-of-time samples scored separately. Binning, feature selection, master-scale boundaries and challenger hyperparameters were all fixed before either out-of-time sample was scored.

4.2 Champion: weight-of-evidence logistic scorecard

Binning. Each numeric feature starts from up to ten quantile bins. Adjacent bins are merged until (a) every bin holds at least 5% of the training sample and (b) the default rate moves monotonically across bins. Monotonicity is imposed because a scorecard that rewards a higher debt-to-income ratio over some range is hard to defend to a credit committee, and the reversal is usually noise. Missing values form their own bin when they cover at least 0.5% of the sample, and otherwise score neutral (WoE = 0) rather than inherit a weight set by a handful of loans. Categories under 2% are pooled. WoE is ln(share of goods / share of bads), so positive WoE means lower risk.

Selection. Features need an information value of at least 0.02. Going down the IV ranking, a feature is dropped if its WoE correlates above 0.6 with one already kept. That leaves avg_cur_bal, total_bc_limit and tot_cur_bal, among others, behind tot_hi_cred_lim and bc_open_to_buy. A logistic regression is then fitted, and any feature with a wrong-signed (positive) coefficient or p > 0.01 is removed one at a time until none remain. None were removed at this stage.

Scaling. Scores are set to 600 points at good:bad odds of 50:1, with 20 points to double the odds. The intercept is shared equally across features, so each bin carries a whole number of points and the points add up to the score (Appendix A).

All coefficients are negative, as required under this WoE convention, and all have |z| > 4. Every feature is a familiar credit-risk driver with an intuitive direction (Figure 7): high FICO, income, credit limits and headroom lower risk; leverage, recent credit-seeking and thin or young files raise it. Two missing-value bins are economically meaningful and kept: no bankcard at all (bc_open_to_buy missing, 15.3% default rate) is high risk, and no enquiry on file (mths_since_recent_inq missing, 9.4%) is the lowest-risk group of that feature.

Master scale. PDs map to eight rating grades with fixed lifetime-PD boundaries: R1 < 4%, R2 4–6%, R3 6–8%, R4 8–11%, R5 11–15%, R6 15–20%, R7 20–27%, R8 ≥ 27%.

4.3 Challenger: LightGBM

The challenger uses all 40 allowlisted features in raw form, with categorical handling for home ownership, verification status and purpose. Seventeen features carry monotone constraints where the direction is not in doubt (FICO, debt-to-income, enquiries, utilisation, income, delinquencies and others; challenger.MONOTONE); a check confirms each constraint holds for every one of 2,000 sampled 2015 loans. Hyperparameters were chosen on a time-based hold-out inside the development window (train to 2013Q3, validate 2013Q4) from a seven-point grid. The grid was flat (hold-out Gini 0.350–0.354), so the choice is not fragile. The best setting, 15 leaves per tree and at least 1,000 loans per leaf, is also the most conservative; early stopping ended at 389 trees.

The scorecard's train–test gap is half a Gini point. LightGBM's is eight: it fits the training sample closely. The test and out-of-time results below are the relevant measures, and they show no decay, so the gap is not a generalisation problem. It is still a reason to prefer the more conservative of the tuned settings when refitting. On the test sample, no master-scale grade is rejected by the binomial or Jeffreys test for either model.

6.3 Stability, and what it missed

Figure 3. Mean predicted PD against observed default rate by issue quarter (top), and the score's population stability index against the training sample (bottom).

The score PSI peaks at 0.026 (2014Q4), far below the 0.10 amber threshold. Feature-level stability (CSI) is green for every feature except verification status (0.17 in 2014, 0.23 in 2015, amber), which reflects a change in Lending Club's income-verification practice. That feature is not in the scorecard (IV 0.003), and it contributes 1.8% of LightGBM's explanatory weight.

This is the central validation finding. The distribution of applicants barely moved, but the default rate given those characteristics did. That kind of drift is invisible to PSI by construction. Plausible causes are a looser macro-credit environment and adverse selection as competition for prime borrowers grew. Neither is observable in the application data. Monitoring that relies on PSI would have reported a healthy model throughout 2014–2015.

6.4 Benchmarking against Lending Club's own grade

Lending Club's sub-grade is a natural benchmark: it is the lender's own risk ranking, built with information this model does not have (the full bureau report, internal fraud and verification checks). On the development window it ranks worse than the scorecard (Gini 0.277 vs 0.310). It improves steadily after that and by 2015 matches LightGBM (0.357). Lending Club rebuilt its model on growing data during this period. A model frozen at 2013 falls behind one that keeps learning, which is a further argument for a defined redevelopment cycle.

6.5 Recalibration test

An intercept-only recalibration is the standard remedy when ranking holds and level drifts. To test it fairly, the shift is fitted on the most recent fully observed year (2014) and applied to the next (2015), as a bank would at an annual review.

At the average realised loss per 2015 default (about $6,500), LightGBM's extra 628 avoided defaults are worth about $4.1 million, 0.11% of the $3.6 billion lent. That is material. Against it, LightGBM's top decline reason is a feature the scorecard does not use 15% of the time, which would need a new adverse-action reason framework. It also spreads weight across many correlated bureau fields (Appendix B), so any single explanation is less stable. The recommendation is to keep the scorecard as champion and run LightGBM alongside it as a challenger and early-warning benchmark. If the gap persists over two further vintages and a reason-code framework is approved, LightGBM should be promoted.

7. Risk parameters and expected loss

Expected loss is PD × LGD × EAD per loan. LGD, EAD and default timing are estimated per master-scale grade on development-window defaults, whose recovery workouts were complete by the data snapshot.

  • EAD at default = amount funded − principal repaid. It is 50% of the amount funded in R1 rising to 64% in R8, because riskier loans default earlier in their amortisation.
  • LGD = 1 − (recoveries − collection fees) / EAD, exposure-weighted: 0.87–0.90 by grade. That is typical for unsecured consumer debt sold to collection buyers. Defaults that ended in a debt settlement (11%) lose less, about 0.70.
  • 12-month share. Of lifetime defaults, 24% (R1) to 41% (R8) miss their first payment within 12 months of issue. This gives an empirical one-year PD for the capital calculation.

8. Stress testing and capital

Downturn calibration. Each 2007–2009 loan is given its sub-grade's development-window default rate as a PD. Those PDs average 14.2%; the crisis vintages actually defaulted at 16.5% (8,235 loans). Holding the sub-grade fixed removes the change in borrower mix and leaves the downturn itself, a shift of +0.19 on the log-odds scale. Crisis-vintage LGD was 5.4% higher than in the development window, which gives the downturn LGD factor. This is an approximate severity: Lending Club re-calibrated its grades over the period.

Downturn PD and LGD 15.9% 0.934 $297.4m 8.21%
Realised 14.9% $273.8m 7.56%

The realised 2015 loss rate lies between the baseline and the downturn scenarios. In loss terms, the 2015 vintage experienced most of a crisis-calibrated stress, in a year without a recession. That is the clearest sign of how far underwriting conditions moved.

Capital. Basel's single-factor (Vasicek) formula for other retail exposures is applied to the one-year PD, with the regulatory asset correlation (3–16%) and downturn LGD. Unexpected loss at 99.9% is $373.5m, 10.3% of the amount funded, on top of one-year expected loss of $135.3m. At 99% it is 6.4%. These are illustrative: there is no maturity adjustment, and EAD is taken at origination.

11. Limitations and assumptions

  • Public data, single lender. Lending Club's applicants, policy and pricing are not a bank's. The method transfers; the parameters do not.
  • Lifetime target. The PD is a 36-month lifetime PD. The 12-month PD used for capital comes from the observed timing of defaults by grade, assuming that timing is stable.
  • Selection. Only approved and funded loans are observed. The model has not been tested on applicants Lending Club declined.
  • No macro variables. The model is through-the-cycle by construction; the stress scenario supplies the cycle.
